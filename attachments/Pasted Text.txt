Yes. Your social work, research, and statistics background fits this well: you can connect technology’s measurable performance with what it actually changes in a person’s life—and trace those effects through families, institutions, and society.

I’ve drafted a Person-Centered Technology Impact Protocol, including a scoring rubric, two worked examples, and a validation plan.

Person_Centered_Technology_Impact_Protocol_v0.1.md⁠￼

The central question is:

“Who benefits, who bears the costs, through which systems, compared with what alternative—and what must change?”

The address you supplied provides the ethical foundation: dignity, responsibility, scientific opportunity, solidarity, and attention to those bearing the greatest consequences. Your research contribution would be translating those commitments into observable outcomes and accountable decisions.

1. Put the person at the center, then trace the pathways

Companies would assess a specific product or deployment—not “technology” in general.

Level	What the evaluation examines
Person	Agency, safety, health, learning, accessibility, and basic needs
Micro	Everyday relationships: family, peers, teachers, caregivers
Meso	Connections between settings—for example, how family and school coordinate support
Exo	Decisions affecting the person indirectly: platform policies, utility planning, procurement
Macro	Inequality, institutions, resource distribution, scientific progress, surveillance, and warfare risks
Time	Immediate, cumulative, delayed, and intergenerational effects

For instance, the pathway might be: a facility increases peak electricity demand → infrastructure costs change → household bills rise → a low-income household has less money for essential needs. Each link needs evidence; the protocol would not assume the whole chain occurred.

2. Evaluate six domains

Across those systems, assess both opportunities and threats to:

* Agency, rights, and access.
* Health, safety, and development.
* Relationships and participation.
* Material security, including food, water, and energy.
* Environment, institutions, and peace.
* Knowledge, discovery, and creativity.

This gives scientific breakthroughs a place alongside risks. However, a promised breakthrough needs a credible pathway to human benefit: who will gain access, when, and at what cost?

Existing frameworks already cover parts of this territory. NIST considers individual and societal impacts; UNESCO explicitly incorporates dignity and environmental sustainability. Your potential contribution is making the ecological pathways, distribution of effects, and affected people’s own priorities operational. It would need a literature review before claiming originality. 

3. Give impacts numbers—but keep benefits and harms separate

For an initial prototype, each impact receives:

Input	Proposed rating
Magnitude	0–4: negligible through major or transformative benefit; negligible through severe harm
Likelihood	1–4: unlikely through very likely within a stated timeframe
Evidence	Reported separately, including missing evidence and uncertainty
Distribution	Which group experiences the effect, and how many people are exposed

A simple provisional calculation is:

Impact priority = 100 × (magnitude × likelihood) ÷ 16

This produces a 0–100 priority score, not a percentage probability or a validated measure of human welfare. Unknown inputs remain unknown.

An entirely hypothetical assessment might show:

Impact	Magnitude	Likelihood	Priority
Greater independence in self-chosen tasks for autistic youth	3	3	Benefit: 56
Sleep disruption from the same product’s notifications	2	3	Harm: 38
Increased household energy burden near a data center	3	3	Harm: 56

I would not subtract harm from benefit to create one “good technology” number. Doing so could conceal serious harm to a small group beneath widespread modest benefits.

Instead, the company receives a profile: benefits, harms, affected groups, evidence gaps, and required action. Credible severe threats to basic needs or rights trigger independent review regardless of the overall benefits.

4. Make affected people part of the measurement

For autistic young people, success should include their own goals, communication preferences, agency, and experiences. More time using a product—or appearing more neurotypical—is not inherently an improvement. Caregiver observations can contribute without replacing the young person’s account. UNICEF’s guidance provides a relevant foundation for this module. 

For households near a data center, measure actual outcomes: utility spending relative to income, interruptions, water conditions, and which residents receive lasting employment benefits.

5. Build credibility through a focused pilot

Start with one application: a specific AI tool used by autistic young people, where your background is especially relevant.

First, co-design the indicators and rating anchors. Then have independent reviewers assess the same cases, examine agreement, and test whether the assessments anticipate subsequent outcomes. A small pilot can refine the tool; it cannot establish broad validity.

GitHub would hold the protocol, scoring code, synthetic examples, version history, and appropriately aggregated findings. Sensitive participant records belong in a secure research system.
